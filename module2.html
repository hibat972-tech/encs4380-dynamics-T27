Module 2 of 4: the same sensor, fed a sine wave

First-order frequency response

Feed a first-order sensor a sine wave and it returns a sine of the same frequency, but smaller and later. How much smaller and how much later depends only on the product \(\omega\tau\).

Theory in two minutes

Sine in, sine out

With input \(x(t)=A\sin\omega t\), after the start-up transient:

\[ y(t)=K A\,M\sin(\omega t+\phi) \]

\(M\) is the amplitude ratio (normalised by \(K\)) and \(\phi\) is the phase shift.

Amplitude ratio and phase

\[ M=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}} \] \[ \phi=-\tan^{-1}(\omega\tau) \]

The output peak comes \(\Delta t = |\phi|/\omega\) seconds after the input peak.

Cut-off frequency

\[ \omega_c=\frac{1}{\tau}\ \text{rad/s},\qquad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}\ \text{Hz} \]

At \(\omega_c\): \(M = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707\) (−3 dB) and \(\phi=-45^\circ\). Far above it, \(M\) falls by 10× per decade (−20 dB/decade) and \(\phi \to -90^\circ\).

Dynamic error

\[ \delta = M-1 \]

To keep \(|\delta|\le\delta_{max}\) for all frequencies up to \(f_{max}\), check the worst case, \(f_{max}\):

\[ \tau_{max}=\frac{\sqrt{1/(1-\delta_{max})^2-1}}{2\pi f_{max}} \]

What to notice

  • Only \(\omega\tau\) matters. Doubling the frequency has exactly the same effect as doubling τ.
  • At low frequency (\(\omega\tau \ll 1\)) the output follows the input almost perfectly. At \(\omega\tau = 1\) it has lost 29% of its amplitude and lags by 45°.
  • Turn on the start-up transient: the output first drifts, then settles into the steady sine after about \(5\tau\). M and φ describe only the settled part.
  • The phase lag never goes past 90°, however high the frequency.

Engineering meaning

  • A sensor is a low-pass filter. To measure a signal faithfully, its highest frequency must sit well below \(f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\).
  • For 5% dynamic error the signal must stay below about \(0.33\,\omega_c\). Measuring up to 100 Hz needs \(\tau \le 0.52\) ms.
  • Phase lag matters even when amplitude error is small: at 50 Hz that same sensor still delays the signal by 9.3°, which can upset a control loop.
