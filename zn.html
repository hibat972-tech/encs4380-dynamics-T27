Ziegler–Nichols tuning

Raise Kp, with Ki = Kd = 0, until the loop just oscillates forever. That gain is the ultimate gain \(K_u\), and the time between peaks is the ultimate period \(P_u\). Two numbers are enough to set a PID controller.

Simulation only. Every number on this page comes from the simulated loop. Nothing here was measured on hardware. On a real robot you would raise Kp slowly until the speed oscillates steadily, read the period from the encoder log, and keep the loop away from the limits.
Method and limitations

The closed-loop method

  1. Set \(K_i=K_d=0\). Increase \(K_p\) until the output oscillates with constant amplitude. This gain is \(K_u\); the period of the oscillation is \(P_u\).
  2. Set the gains from the table below.

Here \(K_u\) is found two ways. Time domain: bisection on \(K_p\), simulating the loop with RK4 and checking whether the peaks grow or decay. Frequency domain: at the frequency where the open-loop phase is −180°, \(K_u=1/|G(j\omega_u)|\) and \(P_u=2\pi/\omega_u\). They should agree.

With \(K_i=K_p/T_i\) and \(K_d=K_pT_d\).

Limitations

  • Not every plant has a \(K_u\). A first-order or second-order plant with P control is stable for every gain. The loop needs at least three lags (for example a plant plus a sensor lag) or a delay before it can oscillate.
  • The rules aim for a fast decay (about a quarter of the amplitude per cycle). They typically give 25% to 50% overshoot, which is often too much.
  • On hardware, driving a machine into sustained oscillation can damage it, and the actuator limits distort the oscillation.
  • Two numbers describe the plant only roughly. Treat the result as a starting point, then fine-tune.
